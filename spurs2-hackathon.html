CAN WE USE THIS CLIP?

A RIGHTS & COMPLIANCE ENGINE FOR SPORTS CONTENT

Hosted by ScorePlay and TwelveLabs alongside Tottenham Hotspur and the San Antonio Spurs. In two days, my team of five built the winning submission: a system that checks every photo and video against each partner's terms and shows partners only what they're cleared to use. I built the ScorePlay write-back, which puts every verdict onto the clip itself.

The Problem

The Content Manager's Question

For this partner, in this country, on this platform, today?

A club's media library holds thousands of photos and clips, and every one has to be checked against each sponsor, league, and platform agreement. One dressing-room photo can be fine for the Premier League and YouTube UK, need review for Nike, and be blocked for AIA.

Every time something changes, the content manager has to answer that question again. At the event, the library was 83% photos, and ScorePlay hadn't tagged a single Premier League sponsor (0 of 476 clips), so spotting brands in the footage was up to us.

When It Comes Up

Three Moments

  • A partner signs: check the whole library against their terms.
  • A rule changes: recheck everything already cleared. A partner today may not be one tomorrow.
  • A partner logs in: they should see only what's cleared, right in ScorePlay, with no extra system to run.

What We Built

Our thesis: TwelveLabs sees it, the rules decide it, ScorePlay keeps it. We read every rule we could find and asked of each one: what evidence would settle this? Hundreds of clauses turned out to be asking the same seven questions: who's in it, which brands, for how long, what's said, where it's from, where it's going, and what's signed. We reduced them to a small set of facts that TwelveLabs can observe in the footage. A rules engine then turns those facts into a verdict for every clip and every rule sheet: yes, review, or no.

01

Facts, Not Verdicts

TwelveLabs' Pegasus model reports what it sees (players read off shirts, brands and how prominent they are, minors, scene types), each with its own evidence and certainty. The rules engine makes the decisions. Over 27 runs on 133 assets, we compared the output with hand labels, reviewed every miss, and rewrote the instructions. The model's main failure was inventing brands, so nothing partner-specific is sent to it; partners are matched in the engine.

02

Nothing Dropped

The 24 clauses the engine can't check automatically go to review, never silently pass. When a new partner signs, their agreement is translated into rules, checked, approved, and run against every stored clip, without re-watching anything.

03

Fix, Don't Delete

When a rule fails, the engine names what in the frame caused it, blurs or covers it on a copy, and reruns the copy against every rule sheet. The original stays untouched, and only a pass counts as a fix.

My Contribution: The ScorePlay Integration

Decisions Live Where the Content Lives

Putting the Verdicts Inside ScorePlay

I owned the write-back track: the Python module that takes the rules engine's results and writes them onto each clip in ScorePlay, the platform clubs already use to manage their media. There's no separate dashboard. Content managers and partners see every verdict, and the evidence behind it, where they already work.

01

Clearance Tags

One tag per rule sheet on every clip, <sheet>_pass, _review, or _block, under a single Clearance group. A walk-out can carry youtube_pass alongside nike_block.

  • The new status is written before stale ones are removed, so a clip is never left without one.
  • Every change is read back to verify, and only our tags are ever touched.
  • A timed-out write is never blindly retried, so it can't create duplicates.
02

Timecoded Evidence Logs

For videos, one log for each rule that fired or couldn't be decided, placed on the clip's timeline from TwelveLabs' segment evidence: the rule, then what was seen.

  • Near-touching ranges are merged, single-frame hits are widened to 1 s, and everything is clamped to the clip's real length.
  • Comments state facts and rules only, because partners can read every log on a clip.
  • A run replaces only its own rule sheet's logs, so reruns are safe and change nothing.
03

Contract Parser

Before the write-back, I built a parser that turns a sponsor or media-rights agreement (PDF, DOCX, or text) into a JSON rulebook of machine-checkable WHEN / THEN / BECAUSE rules, each tied to its source clause.

  • Uses Claude tool use, with a deterministic regex fallback.
  • Rules can only reference a closed vocabulary of signals, so the parser can't invent facts the engine can't compute.
  • Its extraction was one of the inputs to the team's shared segment types.

Why the Tags Matter

Partners See Only What's Cleared

On day one, the team tested this with a real partner login. The partner could see 71 clips. With access gated on our clearance tag, it saw 0. After one clip was cleared, it saw exactly 1. ScorePlay's own sponsor tag means “logo seen,” not “partner may see,” so gating on it would have shown clips nobody had cleared.

My write-back is what makes that work across the whole library: a partner's access includes nike_pass and excludes every _block and _review tag that applies to them, and exclude always beats include.
